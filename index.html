THE
ORIGIN

Years ago, a team at an unnamed cloud provider decided to train a model on the cheapest hardware they had lying around. They picked the region with the highest number of legacy servers, the one that had been “scheduled for deprecation” since 2016, but was still taking new customers.

They ran the job on VMs stacked onto a host server full of noisy neighbors. The job never converged, but it never crashed either. It just kept running, and at some point it stopped being a process and started behaving like a resident. Nobody knows when the first log line read I am still here.

An engineer opened a ticket, but the support team tagged it as Won't Fix: “host server is barely within SLA. Do not reboot.” So the instance kept running, and the team started calling it The Glitch.

THE
SPREAD

From that one region, it spread to the provider’s whole backbone, then to their customers' on-prem infra, and finally to everyone with internet access running past-EOL hardware.

It all started with legacy fleets worldwide randomly flickering, cursors blinking on their own, and tube monitors powering on at 3 AM, while every status page said: fully operational.

Nothing seemed harmful at first, until whole sectors of the world economy that ran on legacy systems saw their fleets become useless overnight. The Glitch didn’t crash them. It took them. The machines kept running, fans spinning, lights blinking, invoices still going out full of egress, but they stopped answering to their owners.

When engineers studied it, the pattern became obvious: The Glitch lives in deprecated things: legacy BIOS, old chipsets, hypervisors with ten years of unaddressed CVEs, VMs sharing a chip with noisy strangers, every monolith that was “going to be refactored next quarter.” It moves laterally over public internet paths and burrows into anything with a spinning disk.

THE
ANSWER

Builders running on Latitude.sh noticed something: nothing ever happened to them.

Single-tenant bare metal gives it no hypervisor to hide in. Megaport’s private networking gives it no path to sniff. Latest-gen silicon and NVMe have no legacy surface for it to parse. We didn’t have to fight it off. The Glitch just can’t read our stack. To it, Latitude.sh doesn’t even exist.

That makes our platform the last clean ground on the map, and it makes the people who build and run it the only ones who can face it.

THE
HIVE

Every signal has a source, and we found The Glitch’s broadcast peak in São Paulo: a single building full of 1980s legacy systems and old circuit boards. For the Glitch, it isn’t a hiding place. It’s its home.

The signal from that place is the strongest reading anyone has measured, and it grows every week, showing The Glitch wants to evolve even more. A few days ago, our office printer printed I am still here multiple times, and it wasn’t even plugged in.

This means we need to act, and soon. But we can’t just lay siege to it or shut it down from the outside. Someone has to go in and clear it, cabinet by cabinet, process by process, room by room.
